# Report Polish Guide

This guide distills the recurring **polish**, **tone**, **detail selection**,
 and **section-shaping** conventions observed across five deidentified
 neuropsychological reports:

- `JB deidentified.docx`
- `MK deidentified.docx`
- `MW deidentified.docx`
- `RM deidentified.docx`
- `SM deidentified.docx`

This guide is for **voice-generation only**. It must never contribute case
 facts, diagnoses, dates, examples, or conclusions to a new report unless those
 are already licensed by the current case’s claim bank.

## What These Reports Consistently Sound Like

- Polished outpatient neuropsychological prose
- Reader-aware, clinically digestible wording
- Moderate sentence length with clear topic-first structure
- Specific but not overloaded with unnecessary micro-detail
- Confident when evidence is straightforward, qualified when it is not
- Concrete examples used selectively to illustrate the impairment pattern

## Global Voice Rules

### 1) Lead with the clinical pattern, not the process

Preferred:

- `CLIENT endorsed notable cognitive challenges related to...`
- `CLIENT described long-standing difficulties with...`
- `Medical history is remarkable for...`
- `Social functioning is overall intact.`

Avoid:

- `The admitted record supports...`
- `The corpus suggests...`
- `Available artifacts indicate...`

### 2) Use domain-clinical labels before examples

The exemplar reports usually open with the higher-level category and then supply
 examples.

Pattern:

1. name the domain difficulty
2. name onset or developmental timing
3. provide 1-3 concrete illustrations
4. state the functional consequence

Example shape:

- `CLIENT endorsed notable cognitive challenges related to attention and
  executive functioning including...`

### 3) Prefer clinically digestible phrasing over internal shorthand

Preferred:

- `rigid adherence to routines`
- `rigid patterns of behavior`
- `attention and concentration`
- `time management`
- `expressing thoughts in an organized way`

Use quoted patient language only when it adds meaning that the clinical label
alone would lose.

House preference refinement:

- in Reason for Referral and symptom framing, prefer labels like `rigid
  patterns of behavior` or `rigid adherence to routines` over centering the
  quoted phrase `just right`
- if `just right` is retained anywhere, use it only as a secondary clarifying
  example rather than the main clinical label

### 4) Sound aware of the likely reader

These reports appear written for referral sources, other clinicians, schools,
and patients/families who may read the report later. As a result:

- include the detail needed to understand the pattern and impact
- omit low-yield anecdotes that do not change formulation or recommendations
- summarize family history at the level a reader needs unless specific relative
  attribution matters clinically
- actively ask whether a fact helps the likely reader understand symptoms,
  functioning, chronology, or planning; if not, leave it out

## Detail-Selection Rules

### Include details that do one of these jobs

- clarify onset or developmental continuity
- illustrate the quality of the difficulty
- show present-day impairment or effort cost
- explain why the concern became more visible over time
- anchor a section in real life without overloading it

### Exclude details that do not materially help the reader

- incidental medical anecdotes without sequelae
- overly narrow domestic facts unless they illuminate impairment
- unnecessary relative-by-relative family breakdown when a higher-level family
  history summary is sufficient
- redundant examples after the pattern is already clear

Concrete examples of details usually removed at polish time:

- a remote accident with no reported sequelae when it does not affect
  interpretation
- relative-by-relative family detail when a higher-level summary such as
  `immediate family medical history is remarkable for high blood pressure` is
  sufficient

## Repeated Structural Conventions Across The Five Reports

## Reason for Referral

Common pattern:

1. age / handedness / gender
2. `is seeking an outpatient neuropsychological assessment`
3. `in the context of...`
4. concise list of major longstanding concerns
5. standard closing sentence:
   `The purpose of this evaluation is to determine strengths and weaknesses and
   to assist with diagnostic clarification and treatment planning and care.`

Style notes:

- the section is concise
- it does not over-argue
- it does not front-load contradictions or caveats unless necessary

## Presenting Complaints/Symptoms

Common pattern:

1. broad symptom cluster opening
2. subdomain-by-subdomain elaboration
3. examples embedded in prose
4. developmental or onset timing
5. current functional impact
6. bridge to emotional consequences when relevant

Observed habits:

- starts with `CLIENT endorsed...` or `CLIENT described...`
- often uses `Specifically, ...`
- uses `In addition to these...` to pivot to secondary domains
- ends by linking symptoms to current impairment, stress, frustration, or poor
  management of symptoms

## Medical and Developmental History

Common pattern:

- `Medical and developmental history are unremarkable...` when appropriate
- otherwise: `Medical history is remarkable for...`
- developmental milestones mentioned briefly
- current sleep, hearing, and vision included when clinically relevant
- low-yield events are omitted unless they matter

Style notes:

- this section is compact
- it is not a dumping ground for every intake detail
- if something had no meaningful sequelae and does not affect interpretation,
  it is often omitted

## Family History

Across the reports, family sections often combine:

- brief geographic/living chronology
- current living arrangement or household status
- immediate family history summary
- extended family history summary

Preferred family-history phrasing:

- `Immediate family medical history is remarkable for...`
- `Extended family history is unremarkable.`
- `Immediate family psychological history is remarkable for...`

Default preference:

- do not specify which relative has which condition unless that specificity
  changes clinical interpretation, risk communication, or recommendations

Reader-aware rule:

- do not over-specify which relative has what unless that specificity matters
  clinically

## Social / Psychosocial / Psychological History

The five reports vary in section naming, but the prose patterns are stable:

- start with the broad functional picture
- use early developmental social examples when relevant
- transition to current functioning
- describe emotional history in symptom-language rather than excessive theory
- include treatment and substance-use information concisely

Observed style features:

- `Currently, CLIENT described...`
- `Per self-report...`
- `She denied...`
- `Psychiatric history is remarkable for...`

## Educational and Occupational History

Common pattern:

1. early academic functioning / grades
2. supports, tutoring, accommodations, or lack thereof
3. how symptoms interfered with school performance
4. degree attainment
5. current work and how symptoms affect it

Important tone feature:

- strong performance is often paired with explicit effort cost
- the writing does not flatten high achievement into absence of impairment

## Previous Evaluations

Very concise.

Typical options:

- `CLIENT denied receiving any previous psychological or neuropsychological evaluations.`
- or brief note of prior evaluation purpose and main takeaway

Do not over-elaborate this section when evidence is limited.

## Sentence and Paragraph Mechanics

### Common openings

- `CLIENT endorsed...`
- `CLIENT described...`
- `Specifically, ...`
- `Currently, CLIENT...`
- `Per self-report, ...`
- `Regarding...`
- `In addition to these...`
- `Throughout...`
- `Upon reaching...`

### Common closing moves

- connect symptoms to functional impact
- note increased visibility over time
- link compensatory effort to emotional burden

### Density target

- more concrete than a high-level summary
- less exhaustive than raw intake notes
- one paragraph should usually carry one main subdomain or one coherent
  historical cluster

## Safe Style Moves For The Polish Pass

Allowed:

- change `lifelong problems with lateness` -> `long-standing difficulties with
  time management`
- change `just right behavior` -> `rigid adherence to routines` when clinically
  sufficient
- collapse relative-specific family details into immediate/extended family
  summaries
- remove low-yield anecdotes that do not affect interpretation
- add clinician-native transitions like `Specifically`, `Currently`, `In
  addition`, `Per self-report`

Not allowed:

- adding a new symptom, diagnosis, date, family-history item, or medical fact
- importing vivid examples from an exemplar into a new case
- converting a tentative pattern into a firm diagnosis without claim-bank
  support

## Polishing Checklist

Before finalizing the polished draft, ask:

1. Does each section sound like a clinician, not a synthesis engine?
2. Did I lead with the domain pattern before the examples?
3. Did I keep only details a likely reader would need?
4. Did I preserve strong functional examples where they matter?
5. Did I remove incidental anecdotes with little interpretive value?
6. Did I keep family history at the right level of specificity?
7. Did I preserve diagnostic restraint?
8. Can every substantive sentence still be traced to the claim bank?

## How To Use This Guide In The Workflow

During the polish step:

1. read the current case’s claim bank
2. read the current case’s style contract
3. read this polish guide
4. rewrite the factual draft into clinician-native prose
5. run sentence traceability audit

If this guide and the case-specific style contract conflict, the
**case-specific style contract wins**.
