## Global Tone and Restraint

The voice is polished, clinically formal, and moderately restrained. It tends to
sound highly organized and report-grounded rather than conversational. The prose
often states concerns directly, then elaborates with concrete examples and
functional consequences. The style is willing to use interpretive linking within
fully evaluative sections, but in the high-confidence referral/history corpus it
remains strongly anchored to what the client, collateral informants, or records
reported.

This voice does not typically sound sparse. Instead, it favors medium-to-long
sentences, explicit transitions, and complete narrative framing. It often uses
qualifying phrases rather than abrupt compression.

## Lexicon Preferences

### Preferred Attribution Verbs

- `described`
- `reported`
- `endorsed`
- `denied`
- `expressed`

In the broader adult+child corpus, `described` and `reported` became even more
prominent than they first appeared in the adult-only sample. `Endorsed` remains
especially common for symptoms and difficulties. `Described` often introduces
narrative detail, examples, or subjective experience.

### Preferred Framing Phrases

- `in the context of`
- `Specifically,`
- `Currently,`
- `Regarding ...`
- `In addition,`
- `Per self-report,`
- `Per mother report,`
- `Per parent report,`
- `Per psychiatrist report,`

### Preferred Functional-Impact Language

- `resulting in ...`
- `contributing to notable impairment ...`
- `negatively impacting ...`
- `significantly negatively impacted ...`
- `difficulty effectively managing ...`

### Phrases to Avoid or Use Sparingly

- casual verbs such as `said` or `talked about`
- highly colloquial phrasing unless directly quoted
- clipped note-style fragments
- sparse bullet-like history prose

## Sentence Structure

Sentence length is typically medium to long. A corpus pass across the 8 reports
yielded an average of about **21.4 words per sentence**, with many sentences
built from one main clause plus one or more clarifying subordinate clauses.

Common features:

- frequent comma-led subordination
- frequent use of parenthetical clarifiers
- regular use of colon-like framing through introductory clauses
- very little reliance on semicolons or em dashes
- recurring sentence openers that guide the reader through symptom clusters
- frequent explicit source framing in child sections

The cadence often follows:

1. broad statement of concern
2. concrete example or symptom detail
3. current or functional consequence

## Paragraph Structure

### Common Paragraph Shapes

- **topic -> examples -> impact**
- **topic -> chronology -> current impairment**
- **topic -> attribution -> detail**
- **general summary -> modality-specific breakdown**
- **domain header -> source-specific elaboration -> impact**

### Typical Opening Behavior

- section opens with a global summary sentence
- subsequent paragraphs often begin with a domain cue such as `Currently,`,
  `Specifically,`, `Regarding ...`, or `Per self-report ...`
- child psychosocial sections often use mini-domain headers such as `Social`,
  `Behavioral`, or `Emotional`

### Typical Closing Behavior

- closes by naming impairment, stress, or management difficulty
- may end by directing the reader to another section for elaboration

### Example Density

Example density is **moderate**. The voice usually provides enough concrete
detail to anchor the concern, but it does not typically pile up many examples in
one sentence without a framing clause.

## Attribution Style

Attribution is explicit whenever it materially affects the strength or source of
the claim. The voice frequently distinguishes among self-report, mother report,
parent report, psychiatrist report, teacher report, tutor report, and records.
It is comfortable using concise source labels inside sentences rather than only
in separate provenance statements.

Typical attribution behavior:

- self-report may remain implicit when the whole paragraph is clearly client
  history
- collateral attribution is usually named explicitly
- child reports use more explicit parent/teacher/tutor attribution than adult reports
- source conflicts are not heavily dramatized, but source identity is preserved
- prior evaluations are summarized briefly and with clear source ownership

## Section Behavior

### Global Section Habits

- section names are conventional and stable
- adult reports consistently use `Reason for Referral`, `Presenting
  Complaints/Symptoms`, `RELEVANT HISTORY`, and `Previous Evaluations`
- child reports consistently omit a separate `Presenting Complaints/Symptoms`
  section and place concerns directly into `RELEVANT HISTORY`
- history content is broken into semantic subsections rather than one long block

### Section-Specific Overlays

#### Reason for Referral

The voice strongly prefers a highly regular opening sentence.

Adult reports often use:

`CLIENT is a [age]-year-old, [handedness] [gender] who is seeking an outpatient neuropsychological assessment in the context of ...`

Child reports instead usually name the child directly and include age in years
and months.

This is followed by a concise concern cluster and a standardized purpose
sentence.

#### Presenting Complaints/Symptoms

This section is stable in adult reports and usually begins with a broad symptom
statement, then expands by domain. Concern clusters are often separated with
clear framing moves such as `In terms of ...`, `Regarding ...`, `Specifically
...`, and `Currently ...`.

#### History

History sections often open with either `... history is unremarkable` or `...
history is remarkable for ...`. They tend to move from summary to specifics.
Family History often begins with birthplace, moves, or current living context
before summarizing family medical or psychological history.

In child reports, history is more explicitly partitioned into developmental,
medical, family, social, behavioral/emotional, and educational domains. The
voice often alternates between summary statements and source-specific paragraphs
using parent, teacher, tutor, or self-report framing.

#### Previous Evaluations

Previous evaluations are handled very briefly. If none, the voice states that
directly. If present, the voice names the evaluator, date, purpose, and limited
takeaway.

## Adult/Child Differences

Adult reports reliably use a separate `Presenting Complaints/Symptoms` section.
Child reports in this corpus do not. Instead, referral concerns are distributed
into the history subsections.

Adult referral openings usually use `CLIENT is a ...`. Child referral openings
usually use the child's name directly, along with an age-in-years-and-months
format.

Child reports also use more explicit collateral-source framing and often split
social/behavioral/emotional content into mini-subdomains.

## Stable vs Optional Style Traits

### Stable Traits

- formulaic adult referral opening sentence
- formulaic child referral opening sentence with name + age-month format
- preference for `described`, `reported`, `endorsed`, and `denied`
- explicit transitional openers
- medium-long sentences with clause-heavy explanation
- paragraph endings that name impact or impairment
- concise prior-evaluation summaries

### Optional / Lower-Confidence Traits

- exact use of `treatment planning and care` vs `treatment planning`
- extent of interpretive linking outside referral/history sections
- stylistic habits in recommendations or impressions not directly reviewed here

## Known Limits

- built from 8 reports
- strongest evidence comes from referral/history surfaces
- should be applied as a **style-only** pass after content has already been
  drafted honestly
